\begin{abstract}
Intentional ingestion of foreign objects (IIFO) is a distinct, increasingly recognised form of self-harm that poses complex management challenges, particularly in detained and psychiatrically vulnerable populations. This systematic review synthesised outcomes following IIFO and examined whether patient motivation, object characteristics, and demographics influence the need for intervention, complications, and mortality. A comprehensive search of major biomedical and psychological databases in January 2025, supplemented by Google Scholar and bibliography screening, identified human studies of intentional ingestion of non-nutritive foreign bodies reporting endoscopic, surgical, conservative, complication, or mortality outcomes. Eligible studies included all designs and ages; accidental or substance ingestion, non-English full texts, animal studies, and reports lacking motivation, object, or outcome data were excluded. Seventy-one case reports (n=71) and three case series (n=90) were included. Pooled outcomes were endoscopy 50\%, surgery 30\%, conservative management 20\%, complications 30\%, and mortality <1\%. Case series populations were uniformly male, detained, and predominantly motivated by protest, with sharp object ingestion common. Protest motivation was associated with reduced odds of surgery, whereas intent-to-harm and psychiatric comorbidity markedly increased surgical risk and accounted for most deaths, particularly in adults aged 40–64. Substantial under-reporting of motivation and outcomes limits precision and underscores the need for standardised prospective reporting.
\end{abstract}
