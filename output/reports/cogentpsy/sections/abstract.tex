\section*{Abstract}
Intentional ingestion of foreign objects (IIFO) presents complex management challenges, particularly in detained or psychiatrically vulnerable populations. This review synthesised evidence on outcomes following IIFO and examined whether patient motivation, object characteristics, or demographics influence the need for intervention, complications, or mortality. A systematic search of seven biomedical and psychological databases, supplemented by Google Scholar, identified human studies of intentional ingestion of non-nutritive foreign bodies across all ages and study designs reporting endoscopic, surgical, or conservative outcomes. Accidental or substance ingestion, non-English full texts, animal studies, and reports lacking motivation, object, or outcome data were excluded. Seventy-one case reports ($n=71$) and three case series ($n=90$) met inclusion criteria. Meta-analysis of proportions summarised management strategies and adverse events, and associations were examined using $\chi^2$ testing and univariate meta-regression. Pooled rates were 50\% endoscopy, 30\% surgery, 20\% conservative management, 30\% complications, and mortality $<1$\%. Case series predominantly described detained men ingesting sharp objects, most commonly in protest. Protest motivation was associated with lower surgical intervention, whereas intent-to-harm and psychiatric comorbidity markedly increased operative risk and mortality. Motivation and mental health status are therefore key determinants of IIFO outcomes and should inform risk stratification and management pathways.
